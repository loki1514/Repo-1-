Proviyaa · founding script · v1
The Proviyaa Script
Every role in the ecosystem, with a named person in it. The story of how work moves between them — from the first sales call to a rider handing over a bag of groceries. Written as a script so that every technical decision that follows can be traced back to a scene, a person, and the thing they were trying to do.
This is an abstract, not a specification. It names no tables, no APIs, no screens by their code. It names people, what they see when they sit down in the morning, and who they hand their work to. The technical requirements are derived from this document in phases — deliberately not here.
How to read it. Section 2 is the cast list — skim it once so the names mean something. Section 3 is the spine: one merchant, nine scenes, from lead to expansion. Section 4 is the same world seen sideways — a working day in every department. Section 5 follows one customer across all three verticals in a single day. Sections 6 and 7 are the connective tissue: how feedback moves between the two platforms, and every handoff in one table.
1 · The world
Five things, and the lines between them
Proviyaa is the platform. It is one product, deployed once, that a merchant configures into the shape of their business. A restaurant, a grocery store and a salon are not three apps — they are three verticals of the same platform, differing in which modules are switched on and which roles exist. Vini POS, already built, is the restaurant vertical.
One Latur is the customer-facing marketplace. A resident of Latur opens it to order food, buy groceries, or book a haircut. It sits in front of every merchant on Proviyaa and presents them as one city's worth of commerce. The customer has one identity across all of it — the same person who ordered biryani on Tuesday is the one buying atta on Wednesday, and the CRM knows that.
OneLatur is the delivery and fulfilment network. Riders, dispatch, zones, fleet. It is deliberately not inside the restaurant or store application — a merchant marks an order ready, and OneLatur takes it from there. The rider has their own app; dispatch has its own console.
The Growth Panel is where merchants are found, sold to, and paid for. Sales, partners, resellers, affiliates and the commissions that flow to them all live here, separate from the operational platform.
The Proviyaa Console is the platform team's own surface — where organizations are created, configured, supported, secured and measured. It is the master-admin side of what already exists.
The two platforms that matter for feedback
Throughout this script, "two platforms" means a specific pair:
Platform one
The Merchant App
What a restaurant owner, a cashier, a chef, a store manager or a stylist uses every day. Every screen here is where a merchant-side person can raise a problem, rate a change, or ask for something — without leaving the work they were doing.
Platform two
The Proviyaa Console
What the platform team uses. Every problem raised on platform one arrives here, tagged with the organization, the screen, the role and the person. Ops triages, product decides, engineering ships, customer success closes the loop back to platform one.
Section 6 describes the loop between them in detail. The principle is simple: a merchant should never have to leave their own app to be heard, and the platform team should never have to guess where a complaint came from.
The three anchor merchants
To keep the script concrete, three real-shaped businesses in Latur are used throughout. They are invented, but every role in them is one from the taxonomy.
* Annapurna Hospitality — a family restaurant group with three outlets: Annapurna Main (Ausa Road), Annapurna Express (a counter near the railway station), and a third, franchised outlet at Ganj Golai. This is the protagonist merchant of the spine.
* Latur Fresh Mart — a two-branch grocery with a small warehouse, selling on One Latur with delivery through OneLatur.
* Glow Studio — a salon with two branches, running bookings, stylists and walk-ins.

	3 · The spine
One merchant, nine scenes, from a dentist's waiting room to a fourth outlet
This is the flow the whole ecosystem exists to run. Annapurna Hospitality goes from a name Tanvi hasn't heard of to a three-outlet organization with a franchisee, live on One Latur, delivered by OneLatur. Every scene ends with a handoff: who passes what to whom, and what appears on the receiving person's dashboard the moment it happens.
The rule every scene obeys. Nothing is handed over by phone call or WhatsApp that the platform could have handed over itself. When Tanvi closes a deal, Aarti does not get a message saying "new client" — she gets the scoped brief, the signed plan and the payment confirmation on her queue, with Tanvi's name on it. The people still talk. But the work moves through the system, so that six months later anyone can see where it went.

Manasi's territory rules put Latur restaurants with Tanvi. The lead appears at the top of Tanvi's list, marked referral, with Farhan's note attached and a suggested first action: call within 24 hours, referrals convert at three times the rate of cold leads.

Scene 3Scoping & commercialsGrowth Panel · Commission PanelDays 7–12
Omkar writes the brief Aarti will build from
Omkar visits all three outlets over two days. The scoping brief he produces is the single most important document in the deal, because it is what the platform team will configure against without ever having met Sunita. It records, per outlet:
* Annapurna Main — 24 tables in three zones, a family hall that needs its own zone manager, a tandoor as a separate kitchen station, reservations on Sundays, delivery through One Latur. Modules: orders, POS, kitchen display, menu, inventory, staff, reservations, delivery.
* Annapurna Express — 8 tables, counter service, no reservations, no captain. One person bills and manages. Modules: orders, POS, kitchen display, menu.
* Annapurna Ganj Golai — franchised to Deepak Bansode. Full restaurant modules, but the franchise organization type, so Deepak sees his outlet and nothing of the group's finance.
He also records the customs: Sunita wants the Sunday thali as a fixed-price bundle on One Latur; she wants the kitchen screen in Marathi; she wants the Zydus corporate contract billed monthly, not per meal. None of these are software features yet — they are requirements the platform team will meet with configuration where they can and flag to Neha where they can't.
Rahul reviews the brief and the plan Tanvi has selected. He approves the quote. Nikhil, in the Commission Panel, sees the deal value firm up and Farhan's provisional commission recalculates against it. Sunita receives the quote with a link to the demo restaurant she saw on Thursday, now branded in Annapurna's red.
Ishaan applied the brand in twenty minutes from Sunita's menu card — the Design Admin does this for every quote above a threshold, because a merchant who sees their own name on the product signs faster.

Scene 5Building the organizationProviyaa ConsoleDays 15–18
Aarti turns a brief into a working restaurant group
Aarti opens the build queue and starts with the organization itself. She creates Annapurna Hospitality as a franchise-type organization with Sunita as owner and Prashant as organization admin. Scene 7Go-live and the first fortnightMerchant App ↔ Proviyaa ConsoleDays 22–36
The loop between the two platforms starts running
Monday, 11am. Annapurna Main takes its first live order. Sameer has the organization pinned on his operations board and watches the first fifty tickets go through. Nothing breaks. At 1:40pm the kitchen screen at Express stops refreshing — the tablet's wifi dropped. Sagar, at the counter, taps report a problem on the frozen screen. It reaches Sameer tagged Annapurna Express · kitchen display · outlet manager before Sagar has finished typing. Sameer calls Sagar; it is the wifi, not the platform; Sameer notes it and Priya adds "check the router at Express" to Tuesday's call.
Over the fortnight the feedback settles into three kinds, and the platform routes each differently:
* Configuration — "the captain shouldn't see POS", "add a station for desserts". Prashant does these himself on the canvas, or Priya walks him through. No engineer involved.
* Defects — "the round-off is wrong on split bills". Sameer reproduces it, confirms it is the platform not the merchant, and it goes to engineering as a bug with the organization's evidence attached. Fixed Thursday; Sameer verifies on Annapurna's own screen; Priya tells Pallavi.
* Product requests — the Marathi kitchen screen, monthly corporate billing. Neha sees these alongside the same request from two other merchants and schedules the first for next month. Priya tells Prashant when, so Ramesh stops asking.
Every one of these is visible to Prashant on the merchant side as a list: raised by whom, on which screen, what happened to it. He does not have to ask. On the platform side, Kavya can already see Annapurna's adoption — which modules are used daily, which have not been opened — and that becomes part of Priya's day-30 call.
Priya's daily feedback call with Prashant runs for the first ten days, then twice a week, then weekly. The feedback control in the merchant app never goes away.

6 · The two platforms
How feedback moves, and why it never gets lost
The example in the brief — sales talks to the customer, the customer pays, admin builds, customer success demos, ops and development troubleshoot with daily feedback captured on two platforms — is Scenes 1 through 7. This section is only about the last part: the loop, once it is running.
What the merchant side captures
Every screen in the Merchant App has one control: report this. It is the same control on the kitchen display, the floor, the till, the stylist's calendar and the rider's job card. When Ramesh presses it on the pass, what leaves the building is not a message — it is a record: Annapurna Hospitality · Annapurna Main · Kitchen display · Kitchen Manager · Ramesh Lokhande · Wednesday 16:02, plus whatever he typed, plus what the screen was showing. He does not choose a category. He does not pick who to send it to. He does not leave the pass.
The organization admin — Prashant — sees every report raised by anyone in his organization, in one list, with its status. That list is the merchant's side of the loop, and it means Prashant never has to ask "did anyone tell them about the font".
What the platform side does with it
Report raised→Console inbox→Sameer triages→one of three doors
Door 1 · configuration
Back to the merchant
It is something the organization can change itself — a role that shouldn't see a screen, a station that needs adding. Priya walks Prashant through it, or does it with him. No engineer. Closed same day.
Door 2 · defect
To engineering, with evidence
Sameer reproduces it on the merchant's own organization. If it is the platform, it goes to engineering carrying the report, the screen, the role and the reproduction. When it ships, Sameer verifies on the same merchant, and Priya tells the person who raised it — by name.
Door 3 · request
To product, counted
It is something the platform does not do yet. Neha sees it beside every other merchant asking for the same thing. When it is scheduled, every merchant who asked is told when. When it ships, they are told it has.
The fourth outcome
Not the platform
Sagar's frozen screen was the wifi. Sameer says so, kindly, and it is closed as "environment". It still counts — if Express reports the wifi three times, Priya raises the router with Prashant.
The daily rhythm
* Every day, first thirty days: Priya and the organization admin, fifteen minutes. What was raised, what happened, what is coming. The list does the agenda.
* Every day, always: Sameer's peak review after lunch and after dinner. Every restaurant, every store, every salon — what slowed, what errored, what was reported during the rush.
* Every week: Neha's theme review. Which requests grew. Which merchants are behind each.
* Every release: Priya closes the loop, by name, to every person whose report is in it. Ramesh finds out the font shipped because someone told him, not because he noticed.
The principle. A merchant should never have to leave their own app to be heard. The platform team should never have to guess where a complaint came from. And nobody — on either side — should have to ask what happened to something they raised.

	8 · What this deliberately leaves out
So the next document knows where to start
This script says what people do and who they hand to. It does not say how. The technical phases will be derived from it, and these are the questions they must answer — listed here so they are not answered by accident in the prose above.
* Whether a guest table session is linked to a known customer, and on what signal. Section 5 assumes a UPI reference is enough; that is a decision with privacy weight.
* What "report this" captures beyond the tag — a screenshot, the last actions, the state of the screen — and how long it is kept.
* The franchise boundary in detail: which group-level numbers a franchisee may see (their own fee, certainly; the group's margin, certainly not; the group's menu, probably).
* How the seeded demo organizations are kept fresh — they are a Growth asset, and Section 4 assumes they are always mid-service.
* Offline. Scene 2 records that Tanvi wrote it down as a gap. It is the one capability a restaurant on unreliable internet will decide on.
* Rule and approval enforcement. Aarti draws "bill over ₹5,000 needs a manager" in Scene 5 and notes it is not enforced. Which rules are enforced, where, is a phase of its own.
* Where OneLatur's promise time comes from, and who is accountable when it is missed — the merchant's packing, the rider, or dispatch.
* The commission model's edge cases: a referral that is also a reseller's merchant; an upsell on a referred account; a merchant who churns inside the clawback window.
* Data residency, retention and audit — Arjun's whole job, described here in one line.
* Multi-city. Every name in this script is in Latur. "One Latur" becoming "One Solapur" is a question this document does not ask.
